# Entry 1 – Paradigms, Methods and Literature Decisions

## What Happened?

At the beginning of the course, I approached my research problem from a predominantly positivist perspective. Since my project involves Bayesian Artificial Intelligence models, probabilistic inference, predictive analytics, and quantitative evaluation metrics, I assumed that the research problem could be addressed entirely through objective measurement and statistical analysis.

During the discussions on research paradigms and particularly the *Paradigm Swap* exercise, I was challenged to reconsider the nature of the problem itself. While the predictive component of citizen insecurity can be analyzed quantitatively, the phenomenon of insecurity is also shaped by subjective perceptions, social trust, fear, and collective behavior. These dimensions are not directly observable through numerical indicators alone.

In parallel, the systematic literature review activity exposed me to interdisciplinary studies combining machine learning, criminology, sociology, and public policy, revealing methodological approaches that I had initially overlooked.

## So What?

This experience fundamentally altered my understanding of the research problem.

Prior to the course, I viewed citizen insecurity primarily as a prediction problem. My objective was to develop Bayesian models capable of estimating the probability of crime occurrence based on historical patterns and contextual variables.

However, the paradigm discussions highlighted a potential blind spot in my thinking. The real-world impact of insecurity extends beyond measurable crime incidents. Citizens often react to perceived insecurity rather than objective crime statistics. Consequently, the social consequences of insecurity emerge from a complex interaction between observable events and subjective interpretations.

This realization led me to recognize that my research contains both positivist and interpretivist dimensions. The Bayesian model itself remains grounded in quantitative reasoning, but the interpretation and application of its outputs occur within social contexts shaped by human perceptions.

The literature review further reinforced this insight. I discovered that many AI-based crime prediction studies emphasize predictive accuracy while paying limited attention to public trust, transparency, and social acceptance. This revealed a recurring gap between technical performance and societal adoption.

## Now What?

As a consequence, I revised my conceptual framework to explicitly acknowledge the socio-technical nature of citizen insecurity.

Rather than positioning the study solely as a predictive modeling exercise, I reframed it as an investigation into how Bayesian Artificial Intelligence can support decision-making under uncertainty in public security contexts.

This shift influenced several methodological decisions. I incorporated discussions of explainability, uncertainty quantification, and decision-support mechanisms into the protocol. I also began considering how model outputs could be communicated responsibly to policymakers and citizens.

An unresolved tension remains. While Bayesian approaches naturally represent uncertainty, I am still exploring how to integrate subjective perceptions of insecurity into a framework that remains scientifically rigorous and computationally tractable.

# Entry 2 – Reproducibility and Scientific Transparency

## What Happened?

The reproducibility unit required documenting workflows, datasets, analytical procedures, and computational environments in a way that would allow independent replication.

Initially, I believed my project was reasonably reproducible because I maintained code repositories and documented datasets. However, the reproducibility exercises exposed numerous implicit assumptions embedded within my workflow.

I realized that many decisions had not been formally documented, including:

* Data-cleaning procedures
* Variable transformations
* Feature engineering choices
* Hyperparameter selection criteria
* Software dependencies

The paper auditing exercise was particularly revealing. Several published AI studies reported impressive performance metrics while providing insufficient information to fully reproduce their results.

## So What?

This experience changed the way I evaluate scientific contributions.

Previously, I tended to judge research quality primarily through novelty and predictive performance. Reproducibility forced me to recognize that scientific credibility depends equally on transparency.

I became aware that hidden decisions can substantially influence outcomes. Two researchers working from the same dataset may produce different conclusions if preprocessing decisions are undocumented.

This insight also led me to reflect critically on AI research culture. There is often significant emphasis on achieving state-of-the-art performance while comparatively little attention is given to reproducibility infrastructure.

I realized that reproducibility is not merely a technical requirement but a cornerstone of scientific integrity.

## Now What?

I implemented several changes in my project.

* All preprocessing procedures are explicitly documented.
* Model configurations and parameter choices are recorded systematically.
* Software environments are reproducible.
* Version control preserves the evolution of the research workflow.

More importantly, I changed how I interpret published findings. I now assess not only reported results but also the transparency of the methodology supporting those results.

An unresolved concern remains regarding data availability. Public security datasets frequently contain sensitive information, creating a tension between transparency and privacy protection. Balancing these competing obligations remains a challenge.

# Entry 3 – Ethics, Bias and Integrity

## What Happened?

The ethics and bias unit introduced frameworks for evaluating fairness, accountability, transparency, and societal impacts in AI systems.

My initial assumption was that Bayesian models inherently reduce uncertainty and therefore support more responsible decision-making. However, the discussions on fairness metrics and algorithmic bias revealed that uncertainty management alone does not eliminate ethical risks.

The Fairness Impossibility Theorem was particularly impactful. Learning that multiple fairness criteria cannot always be satisfied simultaneously challenged my assumption that ethical AI could be achieved through purely technical optimization.

## So What?

This unit significantly expanded my understanding of ethical responsibility in AI research.

Before the course, I viewed ethics largely as a compliance issue involving privacy protection and informed consent. I now recognize that ethical considerations are embedded throughout the modeling lifecycle.

For example:

* Selecting variables
* Defining target outcomes
* Choosing evaluation metrics
* Interpreting results

all involve value judgments.

The Latin American perspective on data sovereignty was especially influential. Discussions regarding historical patterns of knowledge extraction and external control of data resources prompted me to consider who benefits from predictive systems and whose interests may be overlooked.

In the context of citizen insecurity, these concerns are particularly relevant because predictive models can influence:

* Resource allocation
* Surveillance priorities
* Public perceptions

## Now What?

I expanded the ethical framework of my project beyond privacy considerations.

The protocol now includes:

* Bias assessment
* Uncertainty communication
* Explainability
* Responsible deployment practices

I also incorporated fairness evaluation procedures into the model validation strategy.

Perhaps the most important change was recognizing that ethical decisions cannot be delegated entirely to algorithms.

An unresolved challenge concerns balancing predictive performance with fairness objectives. Improving one dimension may reduce performance in another, and determining acceptable trade-offs remains both a technical and moral question.

# Entry 4 – Academic Writing, Peer Review and Scholarly Dialogue

## What Happened?

The final unit focused on scientific communication, peer review, and protocol refinement.

Receiving feedback from peers exposed assumptions and ambiguities that I had not previously recognized. Several reviewers questioned:

* The scope of the project
* The operational definition of citizen insecurity
* The mechanisms linking Bayesian inference to decision-making processes

Initially, I interpreted some critiques as misunderstandings of my intended contribution.

## So What?

After revisiting the feedback, I realized that the reviewers were identifying areas where my writing lacked clarity rather than challenging the value of the research itself.

This experience highlighted an important distinction between understanding a project internally and communicating it effectively to external audiences.

I also discovered that reviewing other students' protocols sharpened my own analytical skills. Identifying methodological weaknesses in their work often revealed similar vulnerabilities in my own project.

The peer-review process transformed my perception of criticism. Rather than viewing critique as an obstacle, I began to see it as an essential mechanism for improving scientific quality.

## Now What?

The protocol underwent substantial revision.

* Research objectives were clarified.
* Conceptual definitions were refined.
* Methodological justifications were strengthened.
* Sections were reorganized to improve coherence and readability.

More importantly, I adopted a different attitude toward scholarly feedback. I now approach criticism as a source of evidence rather than a challenge to personal competence.

An ongoing challenge is determining when to revise in response to feedback and when to defend a methodological decision. Developing this judgment appears to be a central component of doctoral training.

# Final Synthesis – The Evolution of My Thinking

The most significant lesson from this course was recognizing that research is fundamentally a process of disciplined decision-making under uncertainty.

At the beginning of the semester, I viewed my project primarily as a technical exercise involving Bayesian Artificial Intelligence models. By the end of the course, I understood that every stage of the research process—including paradigm selection, methodological design, reproducibility practices, ethical evaluation, and scientific communication—involves choices that shape the validity and impact of the final contribution.

The course revealed that scientific integrity is not limited to avoiding misconduct. It is reflected in everyday decisions regarding:

* Transparency
* Documentation
* Uncertainty communication
* Engagement with criticism

Perhaps the most important transformation was becoming more comfortable with uncertainty itself. Rather than seeking definitive answers immediately, I learned to value open questions, unresolved tensions, and competing interpretations as productive elements of scholarly inquiry.

Moving forward, I intend to continue developing Bayesian Artificial Intelligence frameworks that support decision-making in citizen insecurity contexts while maintaining a strong commitment to:

* Reproducibility
* Ethical responsibility
* Methodological transparency

The course has fundamentally changed how I think about research. I no longer see Artificial Intelligence solely as a predictive technology. I see it as a socio-technical system whose scientific and societal value depends on the integrity of the research process that produces it.
